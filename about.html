About this project

Who made it, why, and what I have at stake.

My name is Scott Rogers. I built Friction & Flow: The Liquidity Loop on my own, as a way to explain how cross-border payments actually work to people who don't have a finance background and shouldn't need one.

I kept trying to explain to friends why moving money across a border is slow and expensive, and I kept losing them at the word “nostro.” So I built the thing I wished I could hand them.

My friends aren't bankers. They swipe a credit card and expect the magic to happen. A lot of people learn better by doing than by reading, so instead of explaining it again, I made something they could play. This game shows what's behind the curtain.

What it is

A free browser game. No signup, no download, no account, no tracking. It runs as a single web page and works on a phone.

You run a money-transfer business against a traditional bank. Every payment forces one decision — correspondent banking or the XRP Ledger — and the game shows the full cost of both, including the cost that never appears on a fee schedule: the capital a firm must park in foreign accounts before a single customer walks in.

It argues both sides on purpose

The closing screen makes the case for incumbent correspondent banking, which still moves the overwhelming majority of the world's cross-border value. The compliance space charges both rails equally, because anti-money-laundering obligations attach to the activity, not the technology. And the game charges slippage on large bridged payments, because market depth is a real constraint.

Anyone who finishes it thinking one rail is free has misread it. That was a design decision, not an oversight.
