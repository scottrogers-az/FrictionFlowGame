What this is, and what it isn't

The CLARITY Act asks regulators and courts to sort digital assets by function — what an asset does, who controls the system it runs on, and whether its value comes from someone's ongoing efforts. Those are mechanical questions before they are legal ones.

This game teaches the mechanics. Play it and you will know, from having done it rather than read it, what a nostro account is, why pre-funded capital is the expensive part of cross-border payments, what settlement finality means, and why a network with no controlling operator behaves differently from one with a company behind it.

It is not a position on the bill. The game argues both sides by design: the closing screen makes the case for incumbent correspondent banking, and the compliance space charges both payment rails equally, because anti-money-laundering obligations attach to the activity, not the technology. Nobody is asked to support or oppose anything.

Scope limit, stated plainly. CLARITY is primarily a market structure bill — who regulates what, and under which registration regime. This game is about payments and settlement. It builds the predicate understanding that market-structure questions rest on. It is not a summary of the bill and should not be used as one.

Where the game meets the statute

The bill's termWhat it turns onWhere the game shows it
Digital commodity — a digital asset whose value is "intrinsically linked to the use of the blockchain"; excludes securities, derivatives and stablecoins Someone has to answer: linked to what use, exactly? Every corridor space. The use is bridging liquidity so a firm doesn't have to park capital in a foreign account before a customer ever shows up.
Mature blockchain system — "not controlled by any person or group of persons under common control," certified to the SEC by the issuer Whether a system has a controlling operator, or whether validators reach agreement without one The Consensus card. Validators agree on transaction order in seconds, with no mining race and no single party sequencing the ledger.
Ancillary asset (Senate text) — a network token whose value "is dependent upon the entrepreneurial or managerial efforts" of an originator or related person Distinguishing an asset held in expectation of someone else's efforts from one used as a settlement instrument The Market Open corner draws exactly this line: price movement across a 3–5 second settlement window is close to irrelevant; across months of holding, it is not.
Settlement finality, and the customer-property and bankruptcy protections When a transfer becomes irreversible, and therefore whose property it is if a firm fails The Deterministic Finality and Atomic Settlement cards. A payment either completes in full or never happened.
DeFi provisions and developer protections Whether an automated protocol with no intermediary can be regulated like one Path-finding and Auto-bridging. The ledger routes through the cheapest chain of trades automatically. There is no desk and no broker.
Illicit finance provisions Whether faster settlement weakens AML and KYC enforcement The Compliance Review corner charges both players, on both rails. Faster settlement changes the plumbing, not the obligation.
Stablecoins — excluded from digital commodity; yield limits addressed separately What these instruments actually compete with The correspondent banking rail: 5.2%–7.7% in fees, 2–5 days, and $200,000 parked per corridor.

The three things a staffer will actually take away

1. The fee is not the interesting number. Cross-border payments average 6.49% globally against a UN target of 3%, and banks as a provider class run near 15%. But the binding constraint is the capital a firm must park in a foreign account before anyone sends anything — often millions per corridor, unusable, earning nothing. In playtesting, a legacy-only strategy had to turn away roughly seven customers per game purely for lack of pre-funded corridors.

2. De-risking is a policy problem hiding in a technical one. When compliance costs exceed revenue, banks close correspondent relationships and whole economies lose access to the payment system. The game has a card for it because it happens.

3. "Use" is a testable claim, not a talking point. The bill's central definitions turn on what an asset does. Ten minutes of play makes that claim concrete enough to interrogate — including where it's weakest. The game charges slippage on large payments, because bridging through a traded asset depends on real market depth, and on a thin pair a large order moves the price against you. That is the honest limit, and it is built into the scoring.

Questions the game equips someone to ask

Non-partisan, and useful regardless of position on the bill.

  1. If digital commodity status turns on value being "intrinsically linked to the use of the blockchain," what evidence of use should a certification have to show — and who audits it?
  2. The mature blockchain system test asks whether a system is controlled by persons under common control. How is control measured — validator concentration, code-change authority, token distribution, or something else?
  3. Under the Senate's ancillary asset presumption, does an asset's treatment change based on how it's used — settled in seconds versus held for years — or only on who issued it?
  4. Where do the customer-property protections sit relative to settlement finality? If a transfer is irreversible at the protocol level but a firm fails mid-transaction, whose asset is it?
  5. If AML obligations attach to the activity rather than the rail, does anything in this bill change an intermediary's existing BSA duties?

Sources for every figure used

Payment costs come from the World Bank's Remittance Prices Worldwide dataset: a 6.49% global average as of 2025, Sub-Saharan Africa near 7.7%, South Asia near 5.2%, banks as a provider class near 15%, against a UN Sustainable Development Goal target of 3%.

Ledger figures come from xrpl.org: a minimum transaction cost of 10 drops (0.00001 XRP) which is destroyed rather than paid to any validator, ledgers validating every 3–5 seconds under normal conditions, and a consensus protocol with no mining or proof-of-work.

Bill terminology is drawn from H.R. 3633 as passed by the House and the Senate substitute text released July 22, 2026. Legislative text changes; verify against the current version before relying on any characterization here.

The $200,000 pre-funding figure is a simplification for playability. Real nostro balances vary widely by corridor and institution.
